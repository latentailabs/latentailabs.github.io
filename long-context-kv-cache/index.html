How AI Reads a Whole Book at Once — Long Context, KV-Cache & The New Tower (Every Number Measured)

A passcode hidden in a 32,768-token book, and the attention heads, memory tricks and position clocks that find it.

By Arun Prabhakaran · Originally published 21 Oct 2026 in Towards AI · 11 min full read

TL;DR

Qwen3-4B found a passcode hidden in a 32,768-token book 20 out of 20 times, in 2.04 seconds. It works because FlashAttention never stores the 2,304 GB attention table, grouped-query attention shrinks the KV-cache to 144 KB per token, RoPE encodes distance, and a handful of retrieval heads do the reading while many heads (63.8% at 2K tokens, about 35% at 32K) park on an attention sink.

  • 2.04 sfull prefill
  • 14.26 GBpeak VRAM added

Summary

Part 3 hides one sentence, “The secret passcode to the Kesharpur Vault is 7492”, in the middle of a 32,768-token document and asks Qwen3-4B to find it on a 96 GB RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU. It answered correctly 20 out of 20 times, reading the book in 2.04 seconds. The “book” is one paragraph block repeated about 92 times, so an A100 re-run (2 Nov 2026) tried real Wikipedia text with 3 passcodes: 30 out of 30 correct.

Standard attention compares every token with every earlier one: 618.49 billion pairs across 1,152 heads, or 2,304 GB of score tables. FlashAttention computes the same result in on-chip tiles; peak memory rose 14.26 GB, of which 9,496 MB is output scores and 4,608 MB is KV-cache. Grouped-query attention cuts the KV-cache from 576 KB to 144 KB per token, and DeepSeek's MLA goes further. RoPE encodes position as 64 rotating clock hands; forcing that clock to wrap dropped retrieval from 100.0% to 0.0%.

On a 2,048-token input, 735 of 1,152 heads (63.8%) mostly attend to the first 4 tokens and the last 64, an attention sink; at 32,768 tokens it is about 35%. Dropping the sink from a sliding window raised perplexity from 1.39 to 151.7 on the repeated text (9.7 to 119 on real text). Floor 29, Head 11 put 98.4% of its attention on the hidden sentence. Switching off 16 retrieval heads gave 8135 in one setup, but the answer stayed correct in 8 of 10 setups. Keeping 9 of 36 layers global would cut the 32K KV-cache by 74.4% by formula; that tower failed the needle at first and passed 5 of 5 in the re-run only when the retrieval-head layers stayed global. A small test, not proof.

Questions this article answers

How can an LLM read a whole book at once?

By never building the full attention table. For a 32,768-token book, Qwen3-4B compares 618.49 billion token pairs across its 1,152 heads, and writing out every score table in bf16 would take 2,304 GB. FlashAttention instead streams small tiles of queries, keys and values through fast on-chip SRAM with an online softmax, giving exactly the same output. On the test GPU, peak memory rose by only 14.26 GB.

What is the KV-cache and why is it so large?

During generation, every token already read leaves a key and a value vector on every layer, kept in GPU memory so they are not recomputed. With classic multi-head attention (32 KV heads), Qwen3-4B would need 576 KB per token, or 18.0 GB for a 32K book. Grouped-query attention lets 32 query heads share 8 KV heads, cutting that to 144 KB per token and 4.5 GB for the same book.

What is an attention sink in transformers?

Softmax makes attention add up to 100%, so idle heads park it on the first tokens, mostly token #0. On a 2,048-token input, 735 of Qwen3-4B's 1,152 heads (63.8%) send over 80% of their focus to the first 4 tokens plus the last 64; at 32,768 tokens about 35% do. A 256-token window without the sinks reached perplexity 151.7 on repeated text (119 on real text); adding 4 sinks back gave about 18.

What are retrieval heads in LLMs?

A small set of attention heads that find and copy facts from long inputs. On a 2,048-token input, Floor 29, Head 11 of Qwen3-4B put 98.4% of its attention on the hidden sentence. Switching off the top 16 retrieval heads dropped confidence in the first digit from 100% to 25.4% and the model answered 8135. Across 10 setups in an A100 re-run, the answer stayed correct 8 times, while 16 random heads never changed it.

How does RoPE let models handle long context?

Rotary position embeddings split each head's 128 numbers into 64 pairs and rotate each pair by an angle that grows with position, so the attention score depends only on the distance between two tokens. In Qwen3-4B the fastest pair turns 57.30° per token, while the slowest needs 5,063,256 tokens for one full turn, using rope_theta = 1,000,000. With YaRN, Qwen3 stretches its window from 32,768 to 131,072 tokens.
